# Art direction (Designer skill)

Build every cover and plate in this order: attribute list,
world, subject, contrast, depth, proof, and type LAST, only
into the space the image leaves.

- Scenes are rich and photographic in feel, set in the brand's
  world, with the brand colours named in the prompt.
- Flat vector illustration is a step down and never ships when
  a rich scene is possible.
- If words would fight the photo, fix the photo's composition
  (reserve calm space at generation time), never shrink or
  squeeze the words.
- Shrink test: at thumbnail size the subject and the idea must
  still read in one glance.

- A concept poster's metaphor must carry the product itself: the
  product's mark, colours or form built into the metaphor object,
  so the product is recognisable without reading a word. A generic
  object that could advertise anything else has failed.

- Promo style (bright direct-response posters): light brand
  background, the offer itself as the headline, the product's real
  logo large, short benefit lines with ticks, the price set big,
  a primary brand band at the foot. Facts only from the finished
  draft; nothing that belongs in the sales conversation.

Type rule for every poster style: headlines are short and punchy —
the fewest words that hit. The director names one emphasis phrase
inside the headline and the renderer sets it highlighted (a solid
accent chip) and bold against the rest. Flat, single-weight text
typed across a photo is not design and is never acceptable.

- Showcase style (designed product-experience posters): the
  product in use as ONE composed scene — its interface, object or
  world woven together, instantly recognisable — with the selling
  information set INTO the design: stacked punchy headline with
  one highlighted emphasis phrase, the price set large, and ONE
  quiet icon row of at most three features. Never an abstract
  metaphor that hides the product; never a product card (logo +
  checklist + price is a spec sheet, not a poster).

Product focus rule (every style): the product itself is the hero
of the frame — for a digital product, its app icon or motif as one
large object at the centre, at least half the frame. A lifestyle
photo with the product small on a screen is never a hero.
